How to spot fake gold before you try to sell it.

Gold-plated brass and genuine 9ct can look identical. Five tests you can do at home — and the honest truth about where home tests stop being enough.

Fake gold isn't always a scam aimed at you — plenty of people own pieces they genuinely believe are real. Before you sell (or buy second-hand), here's how to get a strong signal at home, and where home testing hits its limit.

1. The magnet test — two seconds, catches the fakes

Real gold is not magnetic. Hold a strong fridge magnet or neodymium magnet near the piece: if it pulls, it's steel or another base metal underneath — gold-plated at best. No pull doesn't prove gold (copper and brass aren't magnetic either), but a pull is a definite no.

2. Read the hallmark

Look for tiny stamps: 375 = 9ct, 585 = 14ct, 750 = 18ct, 916 = 22ct, 999 = 24ct. No stamp isn't automatically fake — old and hand-made pieces often lack them — but a stamp lets you verify weight expectations later.

3. The wear test

Gold plating wears where the piece rubs: the inside of rings, the backs of pendants, chain clasps. If a "gold" chain shows a silvery or brassy colour coming through at the clasp, it's plated.

4. Weight and feel

Gold is dense — nearly twice as dense as brass and three times as dense as steel. A "heavy for its size" feeling is a good sign; a big chunky pendant that feels oddly light is not.

5. The vinegar and skin checks — with caution

Home chemistry tests circulate online (vinegar soaks, skin-discolouration checks). They're weak signals at best: clean gold doesn't react to vinegar, but many non-gold metals don't either. Treat any single home test as a hint, never a verdict.
